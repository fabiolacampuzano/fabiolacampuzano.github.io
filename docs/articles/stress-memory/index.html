Why Your Brain Fails You at the Worst Possible Time

Picture this: you’ve studied for hours on end, and then when the time comes for that big exam, your mind suddenly blanks. Most students know this feeling. Your heart pounds, and you reread the first question 5 times. You even decide to start with the last question on the exam to see if you can answer that one. You know you know the material, but why can’t you remember any of it? Under stress, the brain systems that allow us to store, hold, and retrieve information can fail at the exact moment we need them most.

Memory and Anxiety

Memory begins with encoding, or transferring information in a way to be stored in our brain’s memory systems. Anxiety makes this first step of memory encoding unstable. When you’re anxious for an exam while studying, your attention splits between the actual material you need to retain and anxious thoughts like “I’m going to fail this exam.” In the review by Eysenck et al. (2007), the authors used findings from many experiments comparing high-anxiety and low-anxiety participants on tasks such as Stroop, task-switching, and working-memory span. They found that the anxious participants in these studies performed less efficiently because part of their attention was focused away from the task and focused on their worry-related thoughts. Essentially, Eysenck et al. (2007) found that anxiety hurts attentional control, weakening executive function needed to keep focus. This means that instead of focusing on the actual content your exam covers, your brain prioritizes monitoring threats, even if the threat is just an exam. Episodic long-term memory is disrupted at this stage because encoding failures prevent new information from being consolidated into retrievable memories. Since the transfer from working memory to long-term memory depends on attention, anxiety can impair both working memory and the creation of episodic memories.

This stress overload and anxiety also have an effect on working memory. Working memory is the limited-capacity short-term memory system that allows us to temporarily store and manipulate information in our minds (Baddeley, 2010). This is especially important for solving problems, analyzing texts, applying concepts, and basically anything you would need to do during an exam. Under anxiety and stress, your ability to solve that math problem or apply that concept becomes overwhelmed by worry. A meta-analysis by Moran (2016) reviewed working-memory studies that used tasks such as remembering sequences or visual patterns while manipulating information. Across many studies, anxiety predicted poorer performance on working memory tasks, not due to knowing less but because of the mental load that worrisome thoughts create. This means that even highly prepared students might not perform as well as expected due to a reduced working memory capacity.

As the exam gets closer, the anxiety intensifies, and the brain enters survival mode. The amygdala works as the brain’s threat detector. The review by Kim & Diamond (2002) summarizes research on both human and animal subjects showing how acute stress affects the brain. As sudden stress increases activity in the amygdala, the same stress hormones also reduce prefrontal cortex processing and hippocampal plasticity, making it harder to manipulate information, make, and retrieve memories (Kim & Diamond, 2002). In other words, the brain redirects resources away from recalling the information you just studied towards threat responses and basic survival, making the learned material seem just out of reach. This stress both impairs the function of working memory and the retrieval of episodic memories.

In summary, stress before or during studying disrupts encoding, while stress right before or during the exam disrupts retrieval, and stress throughout impairs both encoding and retrieval by reducing hippocampal plasticity.

Folding Under Pressure

Taking an exam is where all of these effects come together. During an exam, the stakes feel high, and the pressure to perform as well or even better than your peers is immense. In one study by Beilock & Carr (2005), participants completed math problems under two conditions, low pressure (control) and high pressure (told they would be compared to peers or tied to a monetary reward). This study showed that people with high working memory capacity, who were also usually the strongest performers, were the most vulnerable to performing poorly when the situation became too stressful (Beilock & Carr, 2005). When stressed, their working memory shifts focus from knowing how to do something to overthinking each step, which increases errors. Moreover, their usual advantage over their peers who were the weakest performers essentially disappeared. Under stress, their decline in performance is not due to a loss of semantic memory, but rather to a collapse of their working memory system.

Not surprisingly, the physical arousal of shallow breaths and a racing heartbeat amplifies this problem further. Even if you encoded the material well enough while studying, anxiety during the exam can cause issues with retrieval. In their review, Vogel & Schwabe (2016) discussed experiments where participants learned word lists and were placed into a stress condition (cold-pressor test or social-evaluation stress) or a neutral condition (control). Many of these studies showed that participants in the stressed condition had impaired retrieval, even though encoding was the same between groups. They found that the stress hormones, like cortisol, can actually disrupt how the hippocampus accesses stored information (Vogel & Schwabe, 2016). Sometimes it’s only after the exam is turned in and your stress levels have dropped that you might realize the silly mistakes you made or suddenly remember crucial information just as you finally leave the exam room.

The “Good” Stress

So, is stress always bad? Not exactly. Some stress is good and can actually improve attention and memory.

Imagine you are studying or learning the material with no stress or urgency to actually remember this information later on, how would you expect to perform? You would likely feel unmotivated or unfocused, making your brain unlikely to prioritize this information later on.

On the other hand, too much stress can overwhelm your brain and impair both working memory and retrieval. When the arousal becomes too much, it can cause you to overthink, worry, and even blank out at the worst times. This relationship between stress and performance was first captured by Robert M. Yerkes and John D. Dodson. According to their model, performance improves as arousal increases but only until a certain point, once stress becomes too high, then performance quickly declines (Dartmouth Undergraduate Journal of Science, 2021). Basically, moderate stress optimally boosts motivation and focuses attention, and too little or too much stress causes performance to be low.

In the study by O’Sullivan (2010), undergraduate students completed surveys that measured their levels of hope, eustress, distress, self-efficacy, and life satisfaction. By analyzing how these factors are related to each other, this study found that students who view stress as motivating challenges report greater self-efficacy (O’Sullivan, 2010). This manageable and positive stress is known as “eustress”, which aligns with the optimal performance point on the Yerkes-Dodson model, where pressure enhances rather than harms learning.

Implications

One implication is how instructors design test materials. Some courses may only have one or two exams that make up a majority of your grade, putting high pressure on students to perform well. This works well for students who are able to remain calm under pressure, while students who are more prone to test-taking anxiety fail even if they know the material better. Shifting toward more frequent or low-stakes assignments, like projects and essays, can measure students’ understanding of the material without triggering cognitive overload and the downsides of stress. Moreover, reducing ambiguity of performance expectations with clear instructions, sample questions, and even grading rubrics can reduce the uncertainty that might activate the amygdala and disrupt working memory. Understanding how stress interacts with working memory, episodic memory, and semantic memory is essential to creating educational environments that support students’ cognitive performance.

Additionally, stressors don’t just come from the classroom. Students who live with an anxiety disorder, experience financial instability, are facing family issues, or are facing other intense stressors are already going to class with elevated levels of stress. The continual exposure to cortisol can weaken hippocampal function over time, making encoding and retrieval harder even before they start studying for an exam. This means that these high-stakes exams unintentionally benefit those with lower baseline stress and disadvantage students who face persistent stressors in their lives, raising important questions for how institutions can level out the playing field for all students. ## What Open Questions Remain? Although multiple research studies show that stress impairs working memory and retrieval, what actually causes some individuals’ working memory to collapse while others barely change? What genetic, environmental, or developmental factors influence how reactive someone’s amygdala and prefrontal cortex is when it comes to stress? Moreover, how do modern-day stressors such as social media pressure, technology mishaps, and even persistent canvas notifications interact with the brain’s memory-stress systems? These constant micro-stressors increase baseline anxiety, making students more susceptible to working-memory failure during exams and other high-stakes assignments. How much do these constant micro-stressors shift the stress-performance curve? Focusing on the modern-day stressors students face is essential for understanding how today’s learning environments are shaping our cognitive performance. Unlike past generations, students today study, learn, and take exams with tons of digital distractions that can activate our brain’s stress response systems. Future research should aim to find how these continuous digital stressors shape memory processes in modern academic environments and whether interventions can help students manage against the mental fatigue these stressors create.
